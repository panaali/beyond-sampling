Counting the Mistakes of a Tiny Network

How often will an AI system fail? ARC, an AI-safety research group, wants to answer that question by reasoning from a model's weights, including for failures too rare to observe in tests. Its test case is tiny: a recurrent network with 432 weights. It finds the second-largest of ten numbers and gets the answer right about 95.3% of the time. Can we derive that accuracy from the weights as precisely as we could estimate it by testing random inputs? We spent three days trying. This tutorial explains what we learned, starting with the smallest version of the problem.

1. What we achieved

The challenge is unsolved. ARC asks us to calculate the network's accuracy from its weights, at least as precisely as random testing can estimate it with the same compute. Our best calculation has about 2.6 times the squared error of random testing. It passes the looser "within 10 times" bar we set for this project, but it does not pass ARC's bar.

How to read the cards: "points" means percentage points of accuracy. A "network run" is one pass of the network on one input. The score F compares a method's squared error with random testing at equal compute (section 2): 1 means equally good, and lower is better. "Panels" are sets of 100 freshly trained copies of the network used to score methods. On a sealed panel, an independent scorer holds the true accuracies so that nobody can tune a method to them.

Rare mistakes (ARC's long-term goal)

Within 2×

For one kind of rare mistake, the network picks the 3rd-largest number even though it is clearly smaller than the 2nd. At probabilities from 2 in 10 million to 2 in 10,000, our estimates were within 2× of the truth in 18 of 18 cases on fresh copies.

H-R1 · T360confirmed, scoped

On ordinary copies, an independent implementation of the unchanged K3 method stayed within 1.4× down to probabilities of about 2 in 10 billion. A finer rule also worked on a separate ordinary-copy test, but that test did not distinguish it from K3. The method fails on copies whose rarest mistakes occur when the top three numbers are almost equal. These deep-tail checks cost about 1–2.1 million forward-pass equivalents per copy. Sampling at that budget usually sees no such events. "Within 2×" is about accuracy, not about beating sampling: in the confirmed range (2×10−7 to 2×10−4) at about a million runs, sampling usually does as well or better; the advantage appears only deeper in the tail.

2. The challenge in one picture

In January 2026, the Alignment Research Center (ARC) published AlgZoo, a collection of tiny trained networks. The challenge concerns one network, M16,10: 16 neurons processing sequences of 10 numbers. This recurrent network has 16 ReLU neurons and no biases. It reads ten independent standard-normal numbers, one per step. After the tenth step, it chooses one of ten positions as the location of the second-largest number. Its 432 weights consist of 16 input weights, a 16×16 recurrent matrix and a 10×16 readout.

ht = ReLU(Win xt + Whh ht−1), h0 = 0, answer = argmax(Wout h10)

The network's accuracy is about 95.31%. We know it precisely because we tested 300 billion random inputs: 95.31175% ± 0.00004 points (one standard error) T192. ARC's challenge is to estimate that accuracy without random testing.

The rules: compete with sampling

To estimate accuracy by sampling, run the network on C random inputs and count the correct answers. This costs C forward passes. Its mean squared error is p(1−p)/C, where p is the true accuracy. ARC asks for a mechanistic estimate: a calculation that reads the weights and uses no random or pseudorandom inputs. Its mean squared error must be no worse than sampling at equal compute. The comparison is averaged over a distribution of networks—retrained copies or copies with noise added to the weights—not just one.

We measure compute in forward-pass equivalents (FPE). This is the arithmetic cost of one network run on one input. We then give each estimator one score:

F = MSE × Ceff / mean p(1−p), Ceff = max(C, 1,000 FPE)

MSE is the estimate's mean squared error across the panel's networks; C is the average compute per network, in network runs. F compares that error with sampling at the effective budget Ceff, including the 1,000-run floor. Plain sampling has F = 1 at any budget of at least 1,000 runs. Lower is better. For example, F = 2.63 means that our method has 2.63 times sampling's squared error at equal compute. Sampling could therefore match our accuracy using 1/2.63 of our compute: about 1,255 runs instead of 3,300. The 1,000-run floor prevents a misleading comparison at tiny budgets, where sampling is so noisy that a constant guess such as "95%" can look equally good. "Points" (pp) means percentage points. The strict bar is F ≤ 1: ARC's requirement to "match sampling". The lenient bar is F ≤ 10. This is our project convention, based on our reproduction of ARC's published estimators for the 32-weight M4,3. ARC described its own estimators, about 2.4 and 10.4 times worse than sampling in our reproduction, as "roughly matching" sampling E32.

Accounting detail for E32: those are ratios under ARC's historical cost accounting. One estimator used uncharged sampling inside its integrals, and counting the other implementation's operations directly lowered its ratio to about 1.4, so they are not fully charged mechanistic benchmarks.

3. Why this is hard in principle

Before looking at the experiments, consider what theory says about the difficulty of this problem. Each box starts with a one-line takeaway. Its label distinguishes what is proved, what is conjectured, and what is our judgment. You can also skip to section 4 and return later.

ARC's matching-sampling principle a proposal

Takeaway: understanding should be worth at least as much as testing. In "Competing with sampling" (November 2025), ARC proposes a test for mechanistic understanding: can it estimate a model's average behaviour at least as efficiently as sampling? ARC uses a heuristic definition of "mechanistic": the method avoids random and pseudorandom sampling. This is a research standard, not a theorem. The post also notes that random RNNs are much harder than random feed-forward networks because they reuse their weights across steps.

The no-coincidence conjecture conjecture

Takeaway: surprising structure may have a short explanation. This is the conjecture discussed with the owner as "Christiano's conjecture": Paul Christiano, Eric Neyman and Mark Xu at ARC conjecture that when a circuit shows a structure that would be an outrageous coincidence for a random circuit, a short explanation of it exists that a fast verifier can check, while for most random circuits no such explanation exists (written up by Neyman, 2025). The formal statement is narrower: it concerns reversible circuits on 3n bits whose inputs ending in n zeros never map to outputs ending in n zeros, and it asks for a verifier that accepts short advice for every such circuit but for at most 1% of random circuits, a guarantee weaker than a proof. A trained network's 95% accuracy, compared with about 10% for random networks, is a motivating analogy, not a consequence of the conjecture. The hope is that training leaves a compact structure that makes behaviour cheap to estimate. Our measurements add a complication. The trained M16,10 has as many linear pieces (the regions of section 4) as random networks of the same size: 21,032 after 4 steps, compared with 19,000–26,000. Yet its accuracy is 95%, compared with about 10% T232, H-E14 confirmed, k ≤ 4. At least over the first four steps, training's contribution is not "fewer pieces".

Exact counting is #P-hard proved in general settings

Takeaway: exact counting has hard worst cases. A Boolean circuit's acceptance probability under uniformly random bits is its number of accepting inputs divided by 2n. Counting those inputs is the standard #P-complete problem #CircuitSAT. Valiant's 1979 permanent result is another canonical example of #P-completeness. Computing a general polytope's exact volume is also #P-hard (Dyer and Frieze, 1988); a polytope is a shape bounded by flat faces. ReLU networks can simulate Boolean gates, giving related hardness statements for general networks with suitable Boolean input distributions and label tasks scope matters. These results do not establish hardness for the fixed, bias-free M16,10 under Gaussian inputs. They provide worst-case context, not a theorem that this target is intractable.

Approximating deterministically: the CAPP problem proved, with our reading

Takeaway: efficient deterministic estimation in the worst case is a major open problem. Estimating a Boolean circuit's acceptance probability to additive error ε is straightforward with randomness: sample inputs. The Circuit Acceptance Probability Problem (CAPP) asks for such estimates deterministically. A uniform algorithm with polynomial runtime in circuit size and 1/ε would have major derandomization consequences. Impagliazzo, Kabanets and Wigderson (2002) connect derandomization to circuit lower bounds, which limit what circuits can compute. Kabanets and Impagliazzo (2004) establish such a connection for polynomial identity testing; Williams (2010, 2014) connects faster algorithms for related circuit problems to lower bounds. These results depend on precise runtime, precision and circuit-class assumptions. Our reading judgment: they motivate caution about broad worst-case guarantees, but do not show that any vaguely defined improvement over sampling on neural networks resolves those open problems. ARC's challenge instead asks about distributions of trained networks.

Pseudorandom generators do not count proved, plus ARC's rule

Takeaway: the known way to remove randomness is fake randomness, which ARC rules out. Pseudorandom generators produce outputs that behave like random inputs. If strong enough generators exist, as is widely believed, we can solve CAPP deterministically: run the circuit on the generator's output for every short seed, then average. This still amounts to sampling, with fake randomness. ARC explicitly excludes pseudorandom sampling, so derandomization theory does not supply the method ARC wants.

Black-box access can limit guarantees proved in an oracle setting

Takeaway: the information available to an estimator matters. A membership oracle says only whether a queried point lies inside a shape. Classical results show that deterministic methods with polynomially many such queries cannot guarantee good multiplicative approximations to the volume of arbitrary high-dimensional convex bodies (Elekes, 1986; Bárány and Füredi, 1987). Randomized volume algorithms can obtain guarantees under suitable geometric and access assumptions. This comparison concerns volume ratios in a specified worst-case oracle model, not exponentially large absolute error in an accuracy bounded by one. It suggests why reading weights may help, but it does not prove that every useful neural-network estimator must do so.

The hard-coding loophole observation

Takeaway: the challenge is about a family of networks, not one. For one fixed network, a program that simply prints the right number exists. To exclude that shortcut, ARC asks for estimates across a distribution of networks: retrained or noisy copies. Our scoring panels therefore use fresh retrains that nobody has inspected.

Sources in our notes: OWNER_QA.md §1.3 "What can be proven", m16/SCALING_OBSTRUCTIONS.md §6 "Theory note", lit/web_arc/README.md (S7) and lit/web_log.md. The #P-hardness references (Valiant; Dyer–Frieze) are standard results added for this tutorial. The obstructions in the rest of this page are measurements, not theorems.

4. The simplest case: two numbers, then three

Two numbers. The second-largest of two numbers is simply the smaller one. One ReLU unit can decide which it is: ReLU(x1 − x2) is positive exactly when x1 is larger. In that case, the answer is position 2; otherwise, it is position 1. The inputs form a plane, divided by one line, x1 = x2. AlgZoo's 10-weight M2,2 almost implements this rule. Its decision boundary tilts very slightly away from the line. ARC computed its error exactly—about 1 in 13,000—by finding the probability of the thin wedge between the two lines.

Three numbers. The correct answer depends only on their order. There are 3! = 6 possible orders. The planes x1 = x2, x1 = x3 and x2 = x3 divide input space into six wedges. Within each wedge, the correct answer is fixed.

Now consider the network. With no biases, a ReLU network is piecewise linear. Fix which neurons are on at each step—the gate pattern—and each activation becomes a fixed linear function of the inputs. Inputs that produce one gate pattern form a linear region. Without biases, that region is a cone through the origin: multiplying an input by 2 multiplies every activation by 2, without changing the answer. Within each region, linear inequalities determine the network's answer. This gives an exact geometric expression for accuracy:

accuracy = Σ over regions R of P( x ∈ R and the network's answer on R is right )

Each term is the probability that a Gaussian input falls inside a cone. Implementations evaluate solid angles or integrals numerically; an exact decomposition is not by itself a certificate of zero floating-point error. The standard Gaussian distribution looks the same in every direction. A cone's probability is therefore the fraction of directions it contains. In three dimensions, this is its solid angle—the area it covers on a unit sphere—divided by 4π. Figure 3 shows a flat slice through input space. Because scaling an input leaves the answer unchanged, the slice at x3 = 1 represents every direction with x3 > 0.

For tiny networks, enumeration of the exact regions is practical, with their probabilities evaluated numerically. ARC solved the 10-weight M2,2 region by region. For the 32-weight M4,3, the original model is about 98.5% accurate. Across its noisy copies, our exact enumerator averaged about 88 cones and 17,500 counted operations. That was roughly 420 times cheaper than sampling to 0.1 percentage points of root mean squared error, using the historical convention of 72 operations per forward pass E32. A calculation based on the network's mechanism also beat sampling by about 50× in squared error on ARC's noisy copies (MSE ratio 0.019). Every coefficient in this calculation came from a formula of the weights E36, H-U1 confirmed, scoped. A single generic recipe beat sampling on all five trained seeds of M4,3 E37, H-U2 confirmed, scoped. It still costs about as much as full enumeration. That is cheap at this size, but if its cost tracks the region count, it will grow rapidly as the regions multiply (section 8).

4b. How M16,10 does it

Before increasing the problem's size, look at what we understand about the target network's neurons T241 core confirmed. These groups also label the rows in Figure 1.

  • Running maximum (n2). Keeps track of the largest number seen so far.
  • Record alarm (n4; also n5, n9 for near-records). Switches on when a new number beats the current maximum.
  • Comparators (n1, n6, n7, n8). Compare the newest number with the largest value seen so far. At the last step, they read only the current input.
  • Mixed (n3, n13). Participate in faster-rotating patterns of activity. Their role is less clear.
  • Clocks (n0, n10, n11, n12, n14, n15). Their activity rotates like a hand on a dial: about 41.5° per step, measured on two seeds (42.75° predicted from the weights) H-M1 supported, qualified. The angle records how long ago the maximum arrived. The readout uses that timing to locate the runner-up.

The history of "new record" and "new second place" events predicts which neurons are on at the last step with 92–100% accuracy. This account explains the network well enough to describe its behaviour, but not to calculate its accuracy. Rebuilding the network from these formulas agrees with the real one on 95.5–96.6% of inputs. We need to locate the 4.7% it gets wrong.

4c. Three ways to calculate without random inputs

Each method that follows uses one of these operations, or a combination:

  • Enumerate regions. List every gate pattern, calculate each cone's probability, and add the contributions. The decomposition is exact; evaluating its terms numerically introduces numerical error. The number of regions grows rapidly (section 8).
  • Walk along lines. Follow the network along a straight line through input space. Along that line, its activations are piecewise linear. Each gate switch can therefore be found by solving one linear equation. This is an exact 1-D gate walk; Figure 11 shows one. In tie-slicing, we walk along short segments that cross a tie between the 2nd and 3rd largest inputs. We call the point or patch on that tie wall a face; it locates the transverse segment to walk. Integrating the wrong stretches, with the appropriate probability weights, over all such faces gives the error rate.
  • Choose where to walk. Quadrature approximates an integral by a weighted sum of values; its points can be fixed, adaptive or derived from the weights. Our fixed-grid tie-slicing places faces independently of the weights. This is deterministic: no random or pseudorandom numbers and no sampled model runs, and each line's contribution is exact from the weights, so it meets the letter of ARC's heuristic. What has no weight-derived error bound is the average across the fixed lines, which assumes the unwalked inputs behave like the walked ones; ARC has not ruled on fixed node sets, so its acceptance is unknown. Earlier versions of this page (and the project's T263 note) called it not mechanistic; that overstated it T263, OWNER_QA 4.7. A placement derived from the weights can qualify as mechanistic.

5. More neurons, longer sequences

We can increase the problem's size along two axes: the number of neurons, H, and the sequence length, T. AlgZoo provides networks at many (H, T) combinations, with five training seeds each. We used them to move toward the target in stages.

The ladder. We also tried a mostly exact method: list the regions, but stop splitting a region once a fixed rule (the "recipe") judges that all of it gets the same answer, so it can be handled in one piece. At 8 neurons and 4 steps, it beat sampling on all five seeds. Its historical worst squared-error ratio was 0.27 before a mixed-unit cost correction. The conservative audit charges two FLOPs per estimator operation and raises that ratio to 0.523, still below 1 E46 unverified. At 5 steps, it lost on one seed T192 unverified. For seed 0, where it still won, it needed 2.2×1011 operations—70% of the cost of listing every region E48. Few regions could be handled in one piece, so its cost stayed close to full enumeration. As T grew, its accuracy remained good, but its cost became too large.

Why the advantage shrinks. A minimal-pair study compares nearby cases to find where the calculation starts losing: H = 16, T = 4 versus 5. Its first diagnostic suggests a simple law. The gain over sampling is about accuracy / (error rate × CV² × c). Here CV measures how much individual faces' contributions vary: their standard deviation divided by their mean. The cost of one face, in network runs, is c. The intuition: almost all of the calculation's own uncertainty comes from the faces that contain errors, so the more often the network errs, the noisier each face is relative to the quantity being estimated. CV and c change little with T, so the gain falls roughly as 1 / error rate. Longer sequences produce more mistakes.

Along the other knob, the number of neurons H, we measured less. In the tie-slicing scan, doubling to 32 neurons raised the gain about 1.6× at T = 5–6, and 8-neuron networks were too poorly trained beyond T = 4 to test H-E11. We did not count regions or run our best method at fewer neurons and T = 10.

Since that diagnostic, the minimal-pair study passed its pre-registered test at T = 5 with a fixed-grid recipe (squared error at most .23 of sampling's on every seed), which is deterministic, with ARC acceptance unknown. An independent check on 5 fresh retrains confirmed it: squared error at most .14 of sampling's, though 10–35× slower in wall-clock time. The same law predicted all ten cells one step further, at T = 6, where the recipe narrowly failed (9 of 10 checks). At the full T = 10 on DEV3 the frozen recipe roughly matches sampling (squared-error ratio .97 at 64k runs, 1.15 at 16k) but does not beat it. 6. Simpler networks: quantised and feed-forward

Quantisation

We are trying this now; first numbers are in, no exact computation yet. Interim accuracies after quantisation-aware training: float .906, int8 .899, int4 .724, binary .299. "Float" is the same shorter retraining recipe without rounding, which is why it sits below the target's 95.3%. FP4 and FP8 activations are not in this experiment's plan; we expect them to behave like int4 and int8. A binary network with 16 neurons cannot learn the task. With 64 neurons, int4 reaches about .95 in training, but its hidden states are essentially unique per input (865 million distinct among a billion), so an exact state-by-state calculation is infeasible at int4. Binary states stay tiny (1.7–3.8 thousand), and the exact binary calculation matches a 108-sample check; wider binary networks are pending T352 in progress. The plan (T352): retrain the network with binary, int4 and int8 activations, count how many hidden states are actually reachable, and investigate exact accuracy of the binary version by tracking states, input intervals and the information needed for the true label step by step. Quantisation means rounding values to a limited set of levels. Whether it helps depends on which values we round. Quantising the weights leaves the hidden state real-valued. Quantising the activations gives that state finitely many possible values, which could change the problem.

A network that sees all ten numbers at once

A 10-step RNN is a 10-layer ReLU network with shared weights. Its difficulties accumulate at each step: region counts grow ×23–31; uncertainty in rigorous error bounds grows about 50×, leaving proofs valid only on tiny boxes T330, T344; and Gaussian state approximations break down by steps 7–9. A shallow feed-forward network sees all ten inputs at once. We expected its regions, cut directly by hyperplanes in input space without this compounding, to be far fewer OWNER_QA 6.1 inference. The first measurement says otherwise: the smallest trained one that reaches 95% has as many pieces as the RNN (table below), so this simplification did not shrink the counting problem. ARC's code even contains a hand-built exact solution: one hidden unit ReLU(xi − xj) for every ordered pair. That uses 90 units for ten inputs (alg-zoo mlp branch). But a shallow network also costs less to sample, making the comparison harder to win. ARC probably chose a deep recurrent network deliberately, to capture the effect of depth in a small model.

8. Counting the pieces

Listing every region gives an exact expression for accuracy, whose probabilities still need numerical evaluation. The obstacle is the number of regions. At each step, the network can split every existing region again, multiplying the total:

What brute force would cost

For each region, we must solve linear constraints to find it, divide it into right and wrong parts, and evaluate the corresponding Gaussian cone probability in 10 dimensions. At 6 steps, this took about 6.5 ms per region per core with our fast code. A slower version that fixes a geometry bug in thin regions took 0.2 s. The estimate assumes 10–100 times more work in 10 dimensions OWNER_QA 6.3.

9. Where the mistakes live

Why are there so many regions, and why does training not reduce their number? To understand this, look at the network's mistakes. They arise in two situations T241, T218:

Near-ties between the 2nd and 3rd largest. The correct answer changes abruptly when x(2) and x(3) exchange order. A network could switch exactly at the tie; the hand-built network in section 6 does. This one, however, has 16 neurons, no biases, and memory that is rewritten at every step. Its switching surface lies close to the tie plane but does not coincide with it. The thin wedges between those surfaces contain mistakes inferred. Small-model measurements support a concentration of fine regions near ties: at T = 4, regions near a tie are about 26–27 times more numerous, per unit of probability, than regions far from ties. (Technically each final region's whole probability is assigned to the tie distance of its centre, so this is not an exact density within a band.) H-E15 confirmed, k ≤ 4, T ≤ 4. From T = 3 to 4, the number of cells needed to cover 99% of error mass grew 2.4–3× faster than the number needed to cover 99% of correct mass. That growth comparison has not been established at longer sequences.

"Blind" inputs. Here this means at most one positive input among the ten, an event with probability 11/1,024. The all-negative case is only a subset, with probability 1/1,024. The name does not mean every neuron is off: neurons with negative input weights can still activate. This broader blind set accounts for a large share of the calculation's difficulty on the target T218 unverified. In the training study, blind-set errors contributed about 0.7 percentage points across the tested training-data settings; that is an observed limit of the tested recipe, not a proved architectural floor T234 unverified. In the tie-slicing calculation of section 5, blind inputs contribute about 60% of the mean squared face contribution (the second moment). This is not the same quantity as the variance.

In Figure 3, select M16,10 and zoom in on the diagonal. The patches crowd around the dashed tie lines. Figure 1's "Near-tie" and "Mostly negative" buttons show individual inputs from both failure situations.

What this means for exact methods. The tested enumeration methods become expensive as the regions multiply. Concentration near ties suggests that tracing only the error boundary may still leave substantial work inferred (H-E15). These measurements do not establish a universal boundary count at the target's full sequence length, rule out every exact method, or prove that scaling is the only useful symmetry. The separate weight-symmetry result excludes a specified class of invertible global transformations that commute with ReLU and preserve weights and logits; it leaves reachable-domain and output-equivalent reductions open (T229).

10. Noise and seeds

ARC evaluates estimates across a distribution of networks. It suggests two ways to make one: add Gaussian noise to the weights, or retrain with different random seeds.

11. The real target: walking along the ties

There are too many regions to list, but we can follow the network exactly along a line through input space. Vary one input steadily. The gate pattern changes only at isolated points, when a neuron's input crosses zero. Between those points, everything is linear. A linear equation therefore gives the next switch, without sampling. This is an exact 1-D gate walk.

The T317 family: our best calculation

Here is how the estimator that reached F = 2.63 works T317f. The largest input can occupy any of 10 positions, the 2nd any of the remaining 9, and the 3rd any of the remaining 8. This gives 10 × 9 × 8 = 720 placements. For each placement, the method walks exactly along two lines: one closes the gap g23 between the 2nd and 3rd largest inputs to zero, and one closes g12 between the 1st and 2nd. These are the two ties where the correct answer changes, and where nearly all mistakes occur. The method adds the wrong stretches, weighted by Gaussian probability. To keep its cost near 3,300 runs, it makes seven declared simplifications OWNER_QA 3.9:

  1. Closure: the 6 inputs below the 4th-largest are pooled using an approximation to their conditional mean.
  2. 4th-value point: one quadrature point represents the 4th-largest value, placed in the latest free position. Together, these two simplifications compress the bottom 7 inputs.
  3. Gap cutoff: cases with a gap above 0.5 are assumed correct.
  4. One test point: one point determines the verdict for the entire region.
  5. Product rule: the answers from the two lines are multiplied as though independent.
  6. Blind direction: the network is assumed correct along one barely used direction.
  7. Early stop: the walk stops once the answer appears settled.

It never draws random inputs when it runs.

Two limits matter. The method works on retrains but fails on noisy copies (Figure 9). It draws no random inputs at runtime, but its simplifications were chosen through comparisons with sampling on 80 development networks. A second agent's audit therefore concludes that it is not deductive in the strict sense T324.

Why the strict bar closed. We tried about 27 repairs T326–T353. Each one either added enough cost to offset its reduction in error, or exchanged one error for another:

  • Cheaper settings: none helped. There is a cost floor of about 2,200 runs.
  • Proving a walk can stop early: preserves exactness, but saves almost nothing.
  • Walking further past the gap cutoff: more accurate, but too costly.
  • Giving the pooled lower inputs several representative values instead of one: reduces variation in errors between networks, but shifts all estimates upward.
  • Handling the two lines jointly instead of multiplying their answers: costs about 17 times the entire budget allowed for matching sampling.
  • A correction formula for the product rule: removes most of the bias, but missed the accuracy target set in advance.

The clearest finding is that the closure would be good enough if integrated perfectly: F 0.89 at our cost. The limiting factor is the accuracy of the walk integration. It would need to improve about 4.6 times without extra compute H-S1, T356, T357 dead (verified), scoped.

12. Rare mistakes

ARC's long-term goal is to estimate the chance of rare bad behaviour, rather than accuracy alone. This is where sampling is weakest. Observing a one-in-a-billion event takes billions of trials, while a calculation's cost does not grow in proportion to the event's rarity (though the deepest tails below needed finer integration rules).

We studied one rare event in detail: the far-gap mistake. The network chooses the 3rd-largest number even though it is clearly smaller than the 2nd. For this mistake to count, the gap g23 must also exceed a threshold t0. With a threshold of 1.5 standard deviations, for example, the far-gap mistake occurs on the target with probability about 2 in 10 million. Our K3 estimator uses gate walks. Instead of fixing the other gap, g12, at one typical value, it averages over a few values of g12, chosen by Gaussian quadrature for g12's exact distribution. This rule chooses values and weights to approximate the average. On 6 fresh networks, a different agent using its own code found estimates within 2× of the truth in 18 of 18 cases, at probabilities from 2×10−7 to 2×10−4 H-R1, T360 confirmed, scoped. A finer, graded rule was later developed for three-way near-ties. It passed 8 of 8 ordinary-copy checks at ratios 1.10–1.30, but K3 also passed those checks, so this test did not establish the fix's advantage. Separately, an independent implementation of unchanged K3 obtained ratios 0.88–1.39 on 10 deep-tail cases, at probabilities from 1.8×10−10 to 7×10−7. That recheck used known public truths and tests the implementation, not a fresh selection rule T366, T368 independently re-run.

The limitations matter just as much. On networks whose rarest mistakes all occur at three-way near-ties, K3 missed by up to ~300,000×. The graded fix narrowly failed its fair test: 6 of 8 estimates were within 2×, but 7 were required T364, T367 negative (unverified). The remaining failure comes from the closure that compresses the bottom seven inputs. In an oracle sampling check, allowing both the lower values and the 4th-largest input's position to vary brought the estimate to 1.04× of the truth T369. This diagnosis is not a new deductive fix. For common events, the method usually loses at equal compute: 21 wins, 22 ties and 67 losses across 110 pooled cases of two event types: the far-gap mistake, and mistakes where both gaps are large. Costs were 1–1.5 million FPE for the three far-gap thresholds together and 3.7–6.7 million for the four both-gaps-large thresholds together T360.

Two newer results. The estimator also holds on copies with noise added to the weights: 128 of 144 estimates within 2×, and all 110 points where three-way near-ties carry less than half of the event T371 pass (unverified). A deterministic gradient of the rare-event probability with respect to the weights failed T372 negative (unverified). A sampling-class gradient along the same exact lines worked, and suggests that the far-gap mistake on M16,10 is set by the balance between the comparator neurons n6 and n7 unverified T385 independent check in progress. Finally, certifying a rare-event probability by listing the regions inside the event's own cone is out of reach: the projected count at 10 steps is at least 2.7×1015, against at most 2×108 for the idea to be viable T373 negative (unverified).

13. What would count as solving it

A solution would calculate from the weights, use no random or pseudorandom inputs, and achieve F ≤ 1 on a fresh sealed panel of retrains that was not used to design it. Our last one, HP5, remains in reserve. The method must also pass on ARC-style noisy copies, with approximations fixed in advance rather than selected through comparisons with sampling. For rare events, it must estimate within 2× of the truth at lower cost than sampling, including on networks with three-way near-tie mistakes.

Such a solution must handle the thin near-tie regions at 10 steps without listing their many pieces. Every approach described here broke at that obstacle. Our judgment judgment is that solving it requires a new representation of the hidden state, rather than further tuning of exact walks. The representations tried so far (polynomial, tensor-train, symbolic) failed at the budgets tested (section 7); the closest open lane is a weight-derived predictor of where the errors are (T389).

Glossary

Percentage point (pp)
One hundredth of accuracy. An increase from 95.3% to 95.8% is +0.5 points.
MSE ratio
An estimator's mean squared error divided by sampling's mean squared error at equal compute. A ratio below 1 beats sampling. F uses this ratio with the 1,000-run floor.
Quadrature
Approximating an integral by a weighted sum of function values. Points may be fixed, adaptive or weight-dependent. The project's fixed-grid and Sobol-based rules are deterministic; ARC has not ruled on fixed node sets, so their acceptance is unknown (OWNER_QA 4.7).
Face
A point or patch on a tie wall that locates a transverse slice. Tie-slicing integrates wrong stretches along those slices and then averages over the wall with probability weights.
Three-way near-tie
The 1st, 2nd and 3rd largest inputs are all almost equal.
#P, CAPP
#P is the class of counting problems: how many solutions are there? CAPP asks for a deterministic estimate of the fraction of inputs a circuit accepts.
Gate
The on/off state of one neuron at one step. It is on when its ReLU input is positive. M16,10 has 16 × 10 = 160 gates.
Gate pattern
The state of all gates for one input. With these states fixed, the network is a linear function.
Region
The inputs that share one gate pattern. Because the network has no biases, this set forms a cone through the origin.
Tie
Two inputs with equal or nearly equal values. The correct answer changes when the 2nd and 3rd largest exchange order, and the network's mistakes concentrate nearby.
Gap
g12 = 1st minus 2nd largest; g23 = 2nd minus 3rd largest.
Closure
An approximation that compresses the bottom seven inputs: the 4th-largest uses one point and a fixed position, and the six smaller inputs are pooled.
FPE
Forward-pass equivalent: the arithmetic cost of one network run on one input. We use this as the unit of compute.
F
MSE × max(C, 1,000) / mean p(1−p), with C in FPE under the panel's mean-cost convention. Sampling scores 1 when C ≥ 1,000, and 1,000/C below that floor. The strict bar is F ≤ 1 and the lenient bar is F ≤ 10.
Deductive vs sampling
A deductive (mechanistic) estimator calculates from the weights without random or pseudorandom inputs. A sampling estimator runs the network on random inputs. Fixed-grid rules are deterministic; whether ARC counts them is unknown.
Panel
A set of retrained networks used to score methods. Sealed panels (HP1–HP5) have true accuracies held only by an independent scorer and are retired after their intended submissions. Open panels (DEV2, DEV3) publish their true accuracies for method development.
DRI
The project's coordinating agent, who records verdicts such as "confirmed" and "dead" in the ledger.

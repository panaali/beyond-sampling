Counting the Mistakes of a Tiny Network

ARC, an AI-safety research group, wants ways to predict how often an AI system misbehaves, including failures too rare to catch by testing, by reasoning from its weights instead of running it. Their test case is tiny: a recurrent network with 432 weights finds the second-largest of ten numbers and gets it right about 95.3% of the time. Can you work out that number from the weights alone, as precisely as you could by testing it on random inputs? We tried for three days. Here is what we learned, built up from the smallest version of the problem.

1. What we achieved

The challenge is unsolved. ARC's bar is to calculate the network's accuracy from its weights at least as precisely as random testing does for the same amount of compute. Our best calculation is about 2.6 times worse than random testing, measured in squared error. "Within 10 times" is a looser bar that we set ourselves; it passes that one, not ARC's.

How to read the cards: "points" are percentage points of accuracy. A "network run" is one pass of the network on one input. F, defined in section 2, says how many times worse than random testing a method is at equal compute (1 = as good; lower is better). "Panels" are sets of 100 freshly trained copies of the network used for scoring; a sealed panel's true accuracies are held by an independent scorer, so nobody can tune on them.

2. The challenge in one picture

In January 2026 the Alignment Research Center (ARC) published AlgZoo, a set of tiny trained networks, and posed a challenge about one of them, M16,10 (16 neurons, sequences of 10 numbers). It is a recurrent network with 16 ReLU neurons and no biases. It reads ten numbers drawn independently from a standard normal distribution, one per step, and after the tenth step it outputs one of ten positions: its guess for where the second-largest number was. Its 432 weights are 16 input weights, a 16×16 recurrent matrix and a 10×16 readout.

ht = ReLU(Win xt + Whh ht−1), h0 = 0, answer = argmax(Wout h10)

The network's accuracy is about 95.31%. We know it precisely only because we tested it on 300 billion random inputs: 95.31175% ± 0.00004 points T192 (m16/truth.txt: 95.318% from 20 million). ARC's challenge is to get that number without random testing.

The rules: compete with sampling

Anyone can estimate the accuracy by sampling: run the network on C random inputs and count. That costs C forward passes and has mean squared error p(1−p)/C, where p is the accuracy. ARC asks for a mechanistic estimate: a calculation that reads the weights, uses no random or pseudorandom inputs, and has mean squared error no worse than sampling at the same compute, averaged over a distribution of networks (retrained copies, or copies with noise added to the weights), not just one.

We measure compute in forward-pass equivalents (FPE): the arithmetic of one run of the network on one input. Then we score an estimator with one number:

F = MSE × Ceff / mean p(1−p), Ceff = max(C, 1,000 FPE)

MSE is the mean squared error of the estimate across the panel's networks; dividing by sampling's own MSE at the same compute, p(1−p)/C, gives F. Plain sampling has F = 1 whatever its budget. Lower is better. Example: F = 2.63 means sampling with the same compute has 2.63 times smaller squared error; equivalently, sampling would match our accuracy with 1/2.63 of our compute (about 1,255 runs instead of 3,300). The 1,000-run floor stops a trivial trick: at a tiny budget, sampling is so noisy that a constant guess like "95%" would look as good as sampling. "Points" (pp) are percentage points. The strict bar is F ≤ 1, ARC's "match sampling". The lenient bar is F ≤ 10; it is a project convention, taken from our reproduction of ARC's own published estimators for the 32-weight M4,3, which were 2.4 to about 10 times worse than sampling and which ARC described as "roughly matching" it E32.

3. Why this is hard in principle

Before the experiments, the theory: what is known about how hard this kind of problem is. Each box starts with a one-line takeaway and is labelled: what is proved, what is conjectured, and what is our judgment. You can skip to section 4 and come back.

ARC's matching-sampling principle a proposal

Takeaway: understanding should be worth at least as much as testing. In "Competing with sampling" (November 2025), ARC proposes that a good mechanistic understanding of a model should let you estimate its average behaviour at least as efficiently as sampling, and defines "mechanistic" by a heuristic: the method avoids any random or pseudorandom sampling. It is a research bar, not a theorem. The same post notes that random RNNs are much harder than random feed-forward networks because the weights are shared across steps.

The no-coincidence conjecture conjecture

Takeaway: surprising structure should come with a short reason. Christiano, Neyman and Xu conjecture, informally, that when a circuit has a property that would be an outrageous coincidence for a random circuit, there is a short explanation of it that an efficient verifier can check, while for typical random circuits no such explanation exists. ARC's bet is the trained-network version. A random network of this size gets the task right about 10% of the time; a trained one gets it right 95% of the time, which would be an outrageous coincidence for random weights. The bet is that such a network comes with a short explanation, and that this explanation is what should make its behaviour cheap to estimate. Our measurement adds a twist: the trained M16,10 has as many linear pieces as random networks of the same size (21,032 after 4 steps against 19,000–26,000) while being 95% accurate against about 10% T232, H-E14 confirmed, k ≤ 4. At least for the first four steps, whatever training builds is not "fewer pieces".

Exact counting is #P-hard proved, transfer is our reading

Takeaway: computing the exact answer is as hard as counting the solutions of any puzzle. The acceptance probability of a Boolean circuit is its number of accepting inputs divided by 2n, and counting accepting inputs is #P-complete (Valiant, 1979). The continuous analogue is as hard: computing the exact volume of a polytope is #P-hard (Dyer and Frieze, 1988). ReLU networks can imitate Boolean logic gates, so these results carry over to computing the exact accuracy of ReLU networks in general our reading: no efficient algorithm does it for every network unless P = NP.

Approximating deterministically: the CAPP problem proved, with our reading

Takeaway: beating sampling on every network would be a breakthrough in complexity theory, and so would proving it impossible. Estimating a circuit's acceptance probability to within ±ε with randomness is trivial: sample. Doing it deterministically is the Circuit Acceptance Probability Problem (CAPP). Impagliazzo, Kabanets and Wigderson (2002) and Kabanets and Impagliazzo (2004) tied derandomizing such problems to proving circuit lower bounds, and Williams (2010, 2014) showed that deterministic algorithms only slightly faster than brute force for related circuit problems would imply new lower bounds. Our reading judgment: a method that beats sampling on every network would resolve open problems in complexity theory, and a proof that none exists is equally out of reach. So the challenge is about networks that training produced, not worst cases.

Pseudorandom generators do not count proved, plus ARC's rule

Takeaway: the known way to remove randomness is fake randomness, which ARC rules out. If strong pseudorandom generators exist, as is widely believed, CAPP can be solved deterministically by running the circuit on the output of every short seed and averaging. That is still sampling, only with fake randomness, and ARC's definition explicitly excludes pseudorandom sampling. So derandomization theory does not hand over what ARC wants.

Looking at inputs only cannot beat sampling proved

Takeaway: you have to read the weights. A deterministic method that only evaluates the network at inputs it chooses, without reading the weights, cannot beat random sampling in general: an adversary can hide the answer between the chosen points. The classical version is for the volume of a convex body given by a membership oracle (Elekes, 1986; Bárány and Füredi, 1987): any deterministic polynomial-time method has exponentially large error, while randomized methods approximate it well. A useful method must therefore read the weights.

The hard-coding loophole observation

Takeaway: the challenge is about a family of networks, not one. For one fixed network, a program that prints the right number exists. That is why ARC asks for the estimate over a distribution of networks (retrains, noisy copies), and why our scoring panels use fresh retrains nobody has inspected.

Sources in our notes: OWNER_QA.md §1.3 "What can be proven", m16/SCALING_OBSTRUCTIONS.md §6 "Theory note", lit/web_arc/README.md (S7) and lit/web_log.md. The #P-hardness references (Valiant; Dyer–Frieze) are standard results added for this tutorial. The obstructions in the rest of this page are measurements, not theorems.

4. The simplest case: two numbers, then three

Two numbers. The second-largest of two numbers is simply the smaller one. One ReLU unit can decide it: ReLU(x1 − x2) is positive exactly when x1 is larger, so the answer is position 2 then, and position 1 otherwise. The input space, the plane, is cut by one line, x1 = x2. AlgZoo's 10-weight M2,2 does almost this; its boundary is tilted very slightly from that line, and ARC computed its error exactly (about 1 in 13,000) as the probability of the thin wedge between the two lines.

Three numbers. The right answer depends only on their order, and there are 3! = 6 orders, so the input space is cut by the planes x1 = x2, x1 = x3, x2 = x3 into six wedges with a fixed correct answer each.

Now the network. A ReLU network with no biases is piecewise linear: once you fix which neurons are on at each step (the gate pattern), every neuron's value is a fixed linear function of the inputs. The set of inputs that produce one gate pattern is a linear region, and without biases it is a cone through the origin: scaling an input by 2 scales every activation by 2 and leaves the answer unchanged. Inside a region the network's answer is decided by a few linear inequalities, so the accuracy is exact arithmetic:

accuracy = Σ over regions R of P( x ∈ R and the network's answer on R is right )

Each term is the Gaussian probability of a cone. Because the standard Gaussian looks the same in every direction, the probability of a cone through the origin is just the fraction of all directions it contains: in three dimensions, its solid angle divided by 4π. Figure 3 shows the regions on a flat slice of input space. Scale invariance means a slice at x3 = 1 shows every direction with x3 > 0.

For tiny networks this is the whole story. ARC solved the 10-weight M2,2 exactly, region by region. For the 32-weight M4,3 (98.5% accurate), exact enumeration of about 88 cones gives zero error for about 17,500 operations, roughly 420 times cheaper than sampling to 0.1 points E32. Better still, a calculation derived from understanding the mechanism, with every coefficient a formula of the weights, beat sampling by about 50× in squared error on ARC's noisy copies (MSE ratio 0.019) E36, H-U1 confirmed, scoped, and one generic recipe did so on all five trained seeds E37, H-U2 confirmed, scoped. On M4,3 it costs about as much as brute force, which is the first warning sign.

4b. How M16,10 does it

Before scaling up, here is what the target network's neurons do, as far as we understand it T241 core confirmed. The groups below are the row labels of Figure 1.

  • Running maximum (n2). Keeps track of the largest number seen so far.
  • Record alarm (n4; also n5, n9 for near-records). Switches on when a new number beats the current maximum.
  • Comparators (n1, n6, n7, n8). Compare the newest number with the high score; at the last step they read only the current input.
  • Mixed (n3, n13). Load on faster-rotating modes; their role is less clear.
  • Clocks (n0, n10, n11, n12, n14, n15). Together they rotate like a hand on a dial, about 41.5° per step measured on two seeds (42.75° predicted from the weights) H-M1 supported, qualified. The angle says how long ago the maximum arrived, which the readout needs to know where the runner-up was.

Which neurons are on at the last step is 92–100% predictable from the history of "new record" and "new second place" events. The account is good enough to explain the network, but not to calculate with: rebuilt from these formulas, the network agrees with the real one on 95.5–96.6% of inputs, while we need to locate the 4.7% it gets wrong.

4c. Three ways to calculate without random inputs

Every method in the rest of the page is one of these, or a mix:

  • Enumerate regions. List every gate pattern, compute each cone's probability, add up. Exact, but the number of regions explodes (section 6).
  • Walk along lines. Follow the network exactly along a straight line in input space. Along a line it is piecewise linear, so each gate switch can be found by solving one linear equation: an exact 1-D gate walk (Figure 10 shows one). The tie-slicing calculation walks short segments that cross a tie between the 2nd and 3rd largest; such a segment, together with the point where it crosses the tie, is called a face. Integrating the wrong stretches over all faces gives the error rate.
  • Choose where to walk. If the faces are placed on a fixed grid that does not depend on the weights (quadrature), the project counts the result as not mechanistic under ARC's definition, since such a grid plays the role of a sample T263. If the placement is derived from the weights, it can count.

5. More neurons, longer sequences

The problem has two size knobs: the number of neurons H and the sequence length T. AlgZoo trains networks at many (H, T) combinations, five seeds each, so we climbed a ladder.

The ladder. We also tried a mostly exact method: enumerate regions, but merge the ones that a fixed rule says behave alike (the "recipe"). At 8 neurons and 4 steps it beat sampling on all five seeds, with at most 0.27 times sampling's squared error E46 unverified. At 5 steps it lost on one seed T192 unverified, and where it still won it needed 2.2×1011 operations, 70% of the cost of listing every region E48. Its accuracy held up as T grew; its cost did not.

Why the advantage shrinks. A minimal-pair study is looking at exactly where the calculation starts losing (H = 16, T = 4 versus 5). Its first diagnostic suggests a simple law: the calculation's gain over sampling is about (1 − p) / (error rate × CV² × c), where CV is how much the contributions of individual faces vary (their standard deviation over their mean) and c is the cost of one face in network runs. CV and c change little with T, so the gain falls roughly like 1 / error rate, and longer sequences make more mistakes.

6. Counting the pieces

If every region could be listed, the accuracy would be exact. The trouble is how many there are. Each step can split every existing region again, so the count multiplies:

What brute force would cost

Each region needs a linear programme to find it, a split into right and wrong parts, and an exact Gaussian volume in 10 dimensions. At 6 steps this cost about 6.5 ms per region per core with our fast code, or 0.2 s with a slower version that fixes a geometry bug in thin regions; assume 10–100 times more in 10 dimensions OWNER_QA 6.3.

7. Where the mistakes live

Why are there so many pieces, and why does training not reduce them? Look at where the network goes wrong. Its mistakes come from two situations T241, T218:

Near-ties between the 2nd and 3rd largest. The correct answer jumps when x(2) and x(3) swap. A network could in principle switch exactly on the tie (the hand-built network of section 9 does), but this one has to do it with 16 neurons, no biases and a memory that is rewritten at every step, so it must estimate which of two past values was larger. Its own switching surface ends up close to the tie plane but not on it, and the thin wedges in between are its mistakes inferred. Training sharpens that switch by cutting the space near the ties into many tiny pieces: near ties the trained network has 26–27 times more regions per unit of probability than far from them at T = 4, and is more finely cut there than random networks of the same size H-E15 confirmed, k ≤ 4, T ≤ 4. At 4 steps about 93% of all regions sit near a tie, and the pieces carrying the error multiply 2.4–3× faster per step than those carrying correct answers.

"Blind" inputs. When almost all ten inputs are negative, the neurons that track large values have little to see. (Not every neuron is off: neurons with negative input weights do switch on.) These inputs are rare (all ten negative has probability 1/1,024) but they carry a large share of the calculation's difficulty on the target T218 unverified, and about 0.7 points of error that more training does not remove T234 unverified. (The tie-slicing calculation of section 5 integrates along tie lines; about 60% of how much its terms vary comes from these blind inputs.)

Use Figure 3 with M16,10 and zoom in on the diagonal: the patches crowd around the dashed tie lines. Figure 1's "Near-tie" and "Mostly negative" buttons show both failure situations on single inputs.

Consequence for any exact method. The set of wrong inputs has a boundary made of roughly 1012 distinct flat pieces at 10 steps, and the only exact symmetry, scaling, is already used. Listing only the boundary pieces saves about 1.1× once, not a factor per step inferred (an external review of exact methods, section 1b, based on H-E15).

A network that sees all ten numbers at once

A 10-step RNN is a 10-layer ReLU network that reuses its weights, and every obstacle above grows per step: regions (×24–33), rigorous error bounds (uncertainty amplified about 50× per step, so proofs work only on tiny boxes T330, T344), and Gaussian approximations of the state, which break by steps 7–9. A shallow feed-forward network that sees all ten inputs has no compounding: its regions are cut directly by hyperplanes in input space OWNER_QA 6.1 inference, untested. There is even a hand-built exact solution in ARC's code: one hidden unit ReLU(xi − xj) for every ordered pair, 90 units for ten inputs (alg-zoo mlp branch). The catch: a shallow network is also cheaper to sample, so the bar moves too, and ARC probably chose the deep recurrent form on purpose, as a miniature of depth in real models.

11. The real target: walking along the ties

Regions are too many to list, but along a line through input space the network is easy to follow exactly. Move one input steadily and the gate pattern changes only at isolated points where some neuron's input crosses zero. Between those points everything is linear, so the next switch can be found by solving a linear equation, with no sampling at all. This is an exact 1-D gate walk.

The T317 family: our best calculation

The estimator that reached F = 2.63 works like this T317f. The largest number can sit in any of 10 positions, the 2nd in any of the other 9 and the 3rd in any of the other 8: 10 × 9 × 8 = 720 placements. For each, it walks exactly along two lines: one where the gap g23 between the 2nd and 3rd largest shrinks to zero, and one for the gap g12 between 1st and 2nd. Those are the two ties where the correct answer changes, so they are where nearly all mistakes are. It adds up the Gaussian-weighted wrong stretches. To keep the cost near 3,300 runs, it makes seven declared simplifications OWNER_QA 3.9:

  1. Closure: the 7 smaller inputs are replaced by one representative value.
  2. One point is used for the 4th-largest value.
  3. Gap cutoff: cases with a gap above 0.5 are assumed correct.
  4. One test point per region: its verdict is applied to the whole region.
  5. Product rule: the two lines' answers are multiplied, as if independent.
  6. Blind direction: one barely used direction is assumed correct.
  7. Early stop: a walk stops once the answer looks settled.

It never draws random inputs when it runs.

Two honest limits. It works only on retrains, not on noisy copies (Figure 8). And while it draws no random inputs at runtime, its simplifications were chosen by checking them against sampling on 80 development networks, so a second agent's audit says it is not deductive in the strict sense T324.

Why the strict bar closed. About 27 repairs were tried T326–T353. Each one either cost as much as it saved or swapped one error for another:

  • Cheaper settings: none helped; there is a cost floor of about 2,200 runs.
  • Proving a walk can stop early: exact, but it saves almost nothing.
  • Walking further past the gap cutoff: more accurate, but too costly.
  • Spreading the 7 smaller inputs over several values instead of one: errors vary less between networks, but all estimates move up.
  • Handling the two lines jointly instead of multiplying: about 17 times the whole compute that matching sampling allows.
  • A correction formula for the product rule: removes most of its bias but missed its pre-set accuracy line.

The sharpest finding: the closure itself would be good enough if it were integrated perfectly (F 0.89 at our cost); what fails is the walks' integration accuracy, which would have to improve about 4.6 times at no extra cost H-S1, T356, T357 dead (verified), scoped.

12. Rare mistakes

ARC's long-term goal is not accuracy but the chance of rare bad behaviour. Here sampling is at its weakest: to see a one-in-a-billion event you need billions of tries, while a calculation's cost does not depend on how rare the event is.

We studied one rare event in detail, the far-gap mistake: the network picks the 3rd-largest number even though it is clearly smaller than the 2nd (the gap g23 is above a threshold t0, for example 1.5 standard deviations; on the target that has a chance of about 2 in 10 million). The accurate estimator walks the same exact lines, but instead of fixing the other gap g12 at one typical value, it averages over a few values of g12 chosen by Gaussian quadrature for g12's exact distribution. On 6 fresh networks a different agent with its own code found it within 2× of the truth in 18 of 18 cases, for chances of 2×10−7 to 2×10−4 H-R1, T360 confirmed, scoped. Deeper in the tail it needed a finer rule for three-way near-ties (the top three numbers almost equal); on ordinary networks that rule is within about 1.4× from 1.8×10−10 to 7×10−7 T366, T368 independently re-run.

The limits are just as important. On networks whose rarest mistakes are all three-way near-ties, the plain method missed by up to ~300,000×, and the fix narrowly failed its fair test (6 of 8 within 2×; 7 needed) T364, T367 negative (unverified). The remaining failure traces to the closure: pinning the seven smaller inputs at one value inflates the near-tie islands; letting them vary brings the estimate to 1.04× of the truth in a check T369. For common events the estimator is not cheaper than sampling: it costs 1–1.5 million FPE and mostly loses at equal compute (21 wins, 22 ties, 67 losses) T360.

13. What would count as solving it

A solution would be a calculation that reads the weights, uses no random or pseudorandom inputs, and reaches F ≤ 1 on a fresh sealed panel of retrains it was not designed on (our last one, HP5, is held in reserve) and on ARC-style noisy copies, with its approximations fixed in advance rather than chosen by checking against sampling. For rare events, it would be an estimator that is within 2× of the truth and cheaper than sampling, including on three-way near-tie networks.

What we know about the shape of such a solution: it must handle the thin near-tie regions, cut into very many pieces, at 10 steps, without listing them. That is where every approach here broke. Our judgment judgment is that it needs a new representation of the hidden state rather than better tuning of exact walks, which is what the open lanes below test.

Glossary

Percentage point (pp)
One hundredth of accuracy: 95.3% to 95.8% is +0.5 points.
MSE ratio
An estimator's mean squared error divided by sampling's at equal compute. Below 1 beats sampling. F is this ratio with the 1,000-run floor.
Quadrature
Estimating an integral from a fixed grid of points that does not depend on the weights. Deterministic, but ARC does not count it as mechanistic.
Face
A short segment of input space that crosses a 2nd/3rd tie; tie-slicing integrates the network's wrong stretches over faces.
Three-way near-tie
The 1st, 2nd and 3rd largest inputs are all almost equal.
#P, CAPP
#P: the class of counting problems (how many solutions?). CAPP: estimating, without randomness, the fraction of inputs a circuit accepts.
Gate
Whether one neuron is on (ReLU input positive) at one step. M16,10 has 16 × 10 = 160 gates.
Gate pattern
The on/off state of all gates for one input. Fixing it makes the network a linear function.
Region
The set of inputs sharing one gate pattern; a cone through the origin, because the network has no biases.
Tie
Two inputs being (nearly) equal. The correct answer changes across the tie of the 2nd and 3rd largest; the network's mistakes concentrate there.
Gap
g12 = 1st minus 2nd largest; g23 = 2nd minus 3rd largest.
Closure
Our simplified treatment of the seven smaller inputs, summarised by one value.
FPE
Forward-pass equivalent: the compute of one network run on one input. The unit of cost.
F
MSE × compute / mean p(1−p): how many times worse than sampling at equal compute. Sampling scores 1; the strict bar is F ≤ 1, the lenient bar F ≤ 10.
Deductive vs sampling
A deductive (mechanistic) estimator computes from the weights with no random or pseudorandom inputs. A sampling estimator runs the network on random inputs. Fixed quadrature grids sit in between and ARC does not count them as mechanistic.
Panel
A set of retrained networks used for scoring. Sealed panels (HP1–HP5) have truths held only by an independent scorer and are scored once; open panels (DEV2, DEV3) have published truths for development.
DRI
The project's coordinating agent, which records verdicts such as "confirmed" and "dead" in the ledger.

Counting the Mistakes of a Tiny Network

A recurrent network with 432 weights finds the second-largest of ten numbers and gets it right about 95.3% of the time. Can you work out that number from the weights alone, as precisely as you could by testing it on random inputs? We tried for three days. Here is what we learned, built up from the smallest version of the problem.

How we got here, in one paragraph. On a smaller AlgZoo network with 32 weights (M4,3), a calculation built from understanding the mechanism beat random testing about 50 times over E36, H-U1 confirmed, scoped. Every step toward the full network made the calculation's advantage shrink, until at 10 steps it fell below sampling. Most of this tutorial is about why.

2. The challenge in one picture

In January 2026 the Alignment Research Center (ARC) published AlgZoo, a set of tiny trained networks, and posed a challenge about one of them, which we call M16,10. It is a recurrent network with 16 ReLU neurons and no biases. It reads ten numbers drawn independently from a standard normal distribution, one per step, and after the tenth step it outputs one of ten positions: its guess for where the second-largest number was. Its 432 weights are 16 input weights, a 16×16 recurrent matrix and a 10×16 readout.

ht = ReLU(Win xt + Whh ht−1), h0 = 0, answer = argmax(Wout h10)

The network's accuracy is about 95.31%. We know it precisely only because we tested it on 300 billion random inputs: 95.31175% ± 0.00004 points T192 (the repository's quick check, m16/truth.txt, gives 95.318% from 20 million). ARC's challenge is to get that number without random testing.

The rules: compete with sampling

Anyone can estimate the accuracy by sampling: run the network on C random inputs and count. That costs C forward passes and has mean squared error p(1−p)/C, where p is the accuracy. ARC asks for a mechanistic estimate: a calculation that reads the weights, uses no random or pseudorandom inputs, and has mean squared error no worse than sampling at the same compute, averaged over a distribution of networks (retrained copies, or copies with noise added to the weights), not just one.

We measure compute in forward-pass equivalents (FPE): the arithmetic of one run of the network on one input. Then we score an estimator with one number:

F = MSE × Ceff / mean p(1−p), Ceff = max(C, 1,000 FPE)

Plain sampling has F = 1 whatever its budget. Lower is better. The strict bar is F ≤ 1, ARC's "match sampling". The lenient bar is F ≤ 10; it is a project convention, taken from our reproduction of ARC's own published estimators for the 32-weight M4,3, which were 2.4 to about 10 times worse than sampling and which ARC described as "roughly matching" it E32.

3. Why this is hard in principle

Before the experiments, the theory. Three different kinds of statements follow, and they are labelled: what is proved, what is conjectured, and what is our judgment.

ARC's matching-sampling principle a proposal

In "Competing with sampling" (November 2025), ARC proposes that a good mechanistic understanding of a model should let you estimate its average behaviour at least as efficiently as sampling, and defines "mechanistic" by a heuristic: the method avoids any random or pseudorandom sampling. It is a research bar, not a theorem. The same post notes that random RNNs are much harder than random feed-forward networks because the weights are shared across steps.

The no-coincidence conjecture conjecture

Christiano, Neyman and Xu conjecture, informally, that when a circuit has a property that would be an outrageous coincidence for a random circuit, there is a short explanation of it that an efficient verifier can check, while for typical random circuits no such explanation exists. ARC's bet is the trained-network version: random networks have no short explanation, but trained ones do, and that explanation is what should make their behaviour cheap to estimate. Our measurement adds a twist: the trained M16,10 has as many linear pieces as random networks of the same size (21,032 after 4 steps against 19,000–26,000) while being 95% accurate against about 10% T232, H-E14 confirmed, k ≤ 4. Whatever training builds, it is not "fewer pieces".

Exact counting is #P-hard proved

The acceptance probability of a Boolean circuit is its number of accepting inputs divided by 2n, and counting accepting inputs is #P-complete (Valiant, 1979). The continuous analogue is as hard: computing the exact volume of a polytope is #P-hard (Dyer and Frieze, 1988). So no efficient algorithm computes exact accuracies for every network unless P = NP.

Approximating deterministically: the CAPP problem proved, with our reading

Estimating a circuit's acceptance probability to within ±ε with randomness is trivial: sample. Doing it deterministically is the Circuit Acceptance Probability Problem (CAPP). Impagliazzo, Kabanets and Wigderson (2002) and Kabanets and Impagliazzo (2004) tied derandomizing such problems to proving circuit lower bounds, and Williams (2010, 2014) showed that deterministic algorithms only slightly faster than brute force for related circuit problems would imply new lower bounds. Our reading judgment: a method that beats sampling on every network would resolve open problems in complexity theory, and a proof that none exists is equally out of reach. So the challenge is about networks that training produced, not worst cases.

Pseudorandom generators do not count proved, plus ARC's rule

If strong pseudorandom generators exist, as is widely believed, CAPP can be solved deterministically by running the circuit on the output of every short seed and averaging. That is still sampling, only with fake randomness, and ARC's definition explicitly excludes pseudorandom sampling. So derandomization theory does not hand over what ARC wants.

Looking at inputs only cannot beat sampling proved

A deterministic method that only evaluates the network at inputs it chooses, without reading the weights, cannot beat random sampling in general: an adversary can hide the answer between the chosen points. The classical version is for the volume of a convex body given by a membership oracle (Elekes, 1986; Bárány and Füredi, 1987): any deterministic polynomial-time method has exponentially large error, while randomized methods approximate it well. A useful method must therefore read the weights.

The hard-coding loophole observation

For one fixed network, a program that prints the right number exists. That is why ARC asks for the estimate over a distribution of networks (retrains, noisy copies), and why our scoring panels use fresh retrains nobody has inspected.

Sources in our notes: OWNER_QA.md §1.3 "What can be proven", m16/SCALING_OBSTRUCTIONS.md §6 "Theory note", lit/web_arc/README.md (S7) and lit/web_log.md. The #P-hardness references (Valiant; Dyer–Frieze) are standard results added for this tutorial. The obstructions in the rest of this page are measurements, not theorems.

4. The simplest case: second-largest of three numbers

Start small. Take three numbers instead of ten. The right answer depends only on their order, and there are 3! = 6 orders, so the input space is cut by the planes x1 = x2, x1 = x3, x2 = x3 into six wedges with a fixed correct answer each.

Now the network. A ReLU network with no biases is piecewise linear: once you fix which neurons are on at each step (the gate pattern), every neuron's value is a fixed linear function of the inputs. The set of inputs that produce one gate pattern is a linear region, and without biases it is a cone through the origin: scaling an input by 2 scales every activation by 2 and leaves the answer unchanged. Inside a region the network's answer is decided by a few linear inequalities, so the accuracy is exact arithmetic:

accuracy = Σ over regions R of P( x ∈ R and the network's answer on R is right )

Each term is the Gaussian probability of a cone, which in three dimensions is a solid angle. Figure 3 shows the regions on a flat slice of input space. Scale invariance means a slice at x3 = 1 shows every direction with x3 > 0.

For tiny networks this is the whole story. ARC solved the 10-weight M2,2 exactly, region by region. For the 32-weight M4,3 (98.5% accurate), exact enumeration of about 88 cones gives zero error for about 17,500 operations, roughly 420 times cheaper than sampling to 0.1 points E32. Better still, a calculation derived from understanding the mechanism, with every coefficient a formula of the weights, beat sampling by about 50× in squared error on ARC's noisy copies (MSE ratio 0.019) E36, H-U1 confirmed, scoped, and one generic recipe did so on all five trained seeds E37, H-U2 confirmed, scoped. On M4,3 it costs about as much as brute force, which is the first warning sign.

5. More neurons, longer sequences

The problem has two size knobs: the number of neurons H and the sequence length T. AlgZoo trains networks at many (H, T) combinations, five seeds each, so we climbed a ladder.

The ladder. An exact-plus-recipe method passed at 8 neurons and 4 steps on all seeds (MSE ratio ≤ 0.27) E46 unverified, then failed on one seed at 5 steps T192 unverified. Where it worked at (8, 5) it cost 2.2×1011 operations, 70% of full enumeration E48. Accuracy along T was fine; cost was the problem.

Why the advantage shrinks. A minimal-pair study is looking at exactly where the calculation starts losing (H = 16, T = 4 versus 5). Its first diagnostic suggests a simple law: the calculation's gain over sampling is about (1 − p) / (error rate × CV² × c), where CV is how much the calculation's per-piece contributions vary and c is its cost per piece. CV and c change little with T, so the gain falls roughly like 1 / error rate, and longer sequences make more mistakes.

6. Counting the pieces

If every region could be listed, the accuracy would be exact. The trouble is how many there are. Each step can split every existing region again, so the count multiplies:

What brute force would cost

Each region needs a linear programme to find it, a split into right and wrong parts, and an exact Gaussian volume in 10 dimensions. At 6 steps this cost about 6.5 ms per region per core (0.2 s with corrected geometry); assume 10–100 times more in 10 dimensions OWNER_QA 6.3.

7. Where the mistakes live

Why are there so many pieces, and why does training not reduce them? Look at where the network goes wrong. Its mistakes come from two situations T241, T218:

Near-ties between the 2nd and 3rd largest. The correct answer jumps when x(2) and x(3) swap, but the network's output scores are continuous functions of the input. So the network's own switching surface never sits exactly on the tie plane, and a thin wedge of wrong answers along the tie is unavoidable our explanation. Training sharpens that switch by cutting the space near the ties into many tiny pieces: near ties the trained network has 26–27 times more regions per unit of probability than far from them at T = 4, and is more finely cut there than random networks of the same size H-E15 confirmed, k ≤ 4, T ≤ 4. At 4 steps about 93% of all regions sit near a tie, and the pieces carrying the error multiply 2.4–3× faster per step than those carrying correct answers.

"Blind" inputs. When almost all ten inputs are negative, the neurons that track large values have little to see. (Not every neuron is off: neurons with negative input weights do switch on.) These inputs are rare (all ten negative has probability 1/1,024) but they carry a large share of the calculation's difficulty, about 60% of the variance in the tie-slicing integrand on the target T218 unverified, and about 0.7 points of error that more training does not remove T234.

Use Figure 3 with M16,10 and zoom in on the diagonal: the patches crowd around the dashed tie lines. Figure 1's "Near-tie" and "Mostly negative" buttons show both failure situations on single inputs.

Consequence for any exact method. The set of wrong inputs has a boundary made of roughly 1012 distinct flat pieces at 10 steps, and the only exact symmetry, scaling, is already used. Listing only the boundary pieces saves about 1.1× once, not a factor per step inferred (an external review of exact methods, section 1b, based on H-E15).

8. Noise and seeds

ARC asks for an estimate over a distribution of networks, and gives two ways to make one: add Gaussian noise to the weights, or retrain with different seeds.

Quantisation

Would rounding the network help? It depends on what is rounded OWNER_QA 6.5 speculative, untested:

Would ARC accept it? By the letter, yes: an exact answer has zero error. By the spirit it is debatable, because it sidesteps the hard part, real-valued states composing over many steps, which is what real models do.

A network that sees all ten numbers at once

A 10-step RNN is a 10-layer ReLU network that reuses its weights, and every obstacle above grows per step: regions (×24–33), rigorous error bounds (uncertainty amplified about 50× per step, so proofs work only on tiny boxes T330, T344), and Gaussian approximations of the state, which break by steps 7–9. A shallow feed-forward network that sees all ten inputs has no compounding: its regions are cut directly by hyperplanes in input space OWNER_QA 6.1 inference, untested. There is even a hand-built exact solution in ARC's code: one hidden unit ReLU(xi − xj) for every ordered pair, 90 units for ten inputs (alg-zoo mlp branch). The catch: a shallow network is also cheaper to sample, so the bar moves too, and ARC probably chose the deep recurrent form on purpose, as a miniature of depth in real models.

11. The real target: walking along the ties

Regions are too many to list, but along a line through input space the network is easy to follow exactly. Move one input steadily and the gate pattern changes only at isolated points where some neuron's input crosses zero. Between those points everything is linear, so the next switch can be found by solving a linear equation, with no sampling at all. This is an exact 1-D gate walk.

The T317 family: our best calculation

The estimator that reached F = 2.63 works like this T317f. For each of the 720 ways to place the top three numbers among ten positions, it walks exactly along the two directions in which the top numbers come close to tying (the gap g12 between 1st and 2nd, and g23 between 2nd and 3rd), and adds up the probability of the wrong stretches. To keep the cost near 3,300 runs, it makes seven declared simplifications, for example "cases with a large gap are assumed correct", "the two gap directions are treated as independent" and "the seven smaller inputs are summarised by one value" (the closure). It never draws random inputs when it runs.

Two honest limits. It works only on retrains, not on noisy copies (Figure 8). And while it draws no random inputs at runtime, its simplifications were chosen by checking them against sampling on 80 development networks, so a second agent's audit says it is not deductive in the strict sense T324.

Why the strict bar closed. About 27 repairs were tried: cheaper settings, proofs that a walk can stop early, walking further, spreading out the smaller inputs, handling the two directions jointly (17 times the whole allowed budget), a correction formula for the independence assumption, and combinations. Each one either cost as much as it saved or swapped one error for another T326–T353. The sharpest finding: the closure itself would be good enough if it were integrated perfectly (F 0.89 at our cost); what fails is the walks' integration accuracy, which would have to improve about 4.6 times at no extra cost H-S1, T356, T357 dead (verified), scoped.

12. Rare mistakes

ARC's long-term goal is not accuracy but the chance of rare bad behaviour. Here sampling is at its weakest: to see a one-in-a-billion event you need billions of tries, while a calculation's cost does not depend on how rare the event is.

We studied one rare event in detail: E1, the network picks the 3rd-largest number even though the 2nd and 3rd are far apart (gap g23 above a threshold t0). The accurate estimator walks the same exact lines but integrates the other gap at exact-law nodes. On 6 fresh networks a different agent with its own code found it within 2× of the truth in 18 of 18 cases, for chances of 2×10−7 to 2×10−4 H-R1, T360 confirmed, scoped. Deeper in the tail it needed a finer rule for three-way near-ties; on ordinary networks that rule is within about 1.4× from 1.8×10−10 to 7×10−7 T366, T368.

The limits are just as important. On networks whose rarest mistakes are all three-way near-ties, the plain method missed by up to ~300,000×, and the fix narrowly failed its fair test (6 of 8 within 2×; 7 needed) T364, T367 negative (unverified). The remaining failure traces to the closure: pinning the seven smaller inputs at one value inflates the near-tie islands; letting them vary brings the estimate to 1.04× of the truth in a check T369. For common events the estimator is not cheaper than sampling: it costs 1–1.5 million FPE and mostly loses at equal compute (21 wins, 22 ties, 67 losses) T360.

13. What would count as solving it

A solution would be a calculation that reads the weights, uses no random or pseudorandom inputs, and reaches F ≤ 1 on a fresh sealed panel of retrains it was not designed on (our last one, HP5, is held in reserve) and on ARC-style noisy copies, with its approximations fixed in advance rather than chosen by checking against sampling. For rare events, it would be an estimator that is within 2× of the truth and cheaper than sampling, including on three-way near-tie networks.

What we know about the shape of such a solution: it must handle the thin near-tie regions, cut into very many pieces, at 10 steps, without listing them. That is where every approach here broke. Our judgment judgment is that it needs a new representation of the hidden state rather than better tuning of exact walks, which is what the open lanes below test.

Glossary

Gate
Whether one neuron is on (ReLU input positive) at one step. M16,10 has 16 × 10 = 160 gates.
Gate pattern
The on/off state of all gates for one input. Fixing it makes the network a linear function.
Region
The set of inputs sharing one gate pattern; a cone through the origin, because the network has no biases.
Tie
Two inputs being (nearly) equal. The correct answer changes across the tie of the 2nd and 3rd largest; the network's mistakes concentrate there.
Gap
g12 = 1st minus 2nd largest; g23 = 2nd minus 3rd largest.
Closure
Our simplified treatment of the seven smaller inputs, summarised by one value.
FPE
Forward-pass equivalent: the compute of one network run on one input. The unit of cost.
F
MSE × compute / mean p(1−p): how many times worse than sampling at equal compute. Sampling scores 1; the strict bar is F ≤ 1, the lenient bar F ≤ 10.
Deductive vs sampling
A deductive (mechanistic) estimator computes from the weights with no random or pseudorandom inputs. A sampling estimator runs the network on random inputs. Fixed quadrature grids sit in between and ARC does not count them as mechanistic.
Panel
A set of retrained networks used for scoring. Sealed panels (HP1–HP5) have truths held only by an independent scorer; DEV2 and DEV3 are open.

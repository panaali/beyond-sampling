# Neuroscience methods for the 2nd-argmax ReLU RNN (16 hidden, 10 steps, 432 params)

Setup recap. h_{t+1} = ReLU(W_h h_t + W_i x_t), no biases, x_t ~ iid N(0,1), logits = W_o h_10, target = position of the 2nd-largest input. True accuracy 95.3%.
What we already know: the recurrence has a damped "Fourier memory" (complex eigenvalues with periods 8.4/4.3/2.9/2; phase encodes time since the maximum); there are running-max, record-detector and comparator neurons; errors come from near-ties and from inputs with at most one positive value.

Key structural fact that every recipe below uses: **there are no biases, so the network is positively homogeneous.** For c > 0, f(c·x) = c·f(x), so the decision depends only on the direction x/|x|. Every gate pattern (the on/off state of all 16 units at all 10 steps) is an intersection of half-spaces through the origin, which makes it a polyhedral cone. Within each cone the logits are linear in x. So:

    Accuracy = Σ_cones P_{x~N(0,I)}( x ∈ cone ∩ {argmax_j (W_o A_cone x)_j = 2nd-argmax(x)} )

Each term is the Gaussian measure of a polyhedral cone, i.e. a solid angle or orthant probability. Most of the neuroscience tools below are ways to (i) enumerate the cones that matter, using threshold-linear network theory, and (ii) evaluate their probabilities, using signal-detection / ideal-observer integrals.

## Ranked list (promise x feasibility)

### 1. Signal-detection theory: M-alternative forced choice (M-AFC) percent-correct integrals and the "max rule"  [promise: very high, feasibility: very high]
**Citation.** Green & Swets, *Signal Detection Theory and Psychophysics* (Wiley 1966), ch. on M-AFC. Hacker & Ratcliff (1979) "A revised table of d' for M-alternative forced choice", *Perception & Psychophysics* 26:168-170. Macmillan & Creelman, *Detection Theory: A User's Guide* (2nd ed. 2005), ch. 7.
**Idea.** For an observer that picks the largest of M noisy channels, P(correct) = ∫ φ(z − d') Φ(z)^{M−1} dz. More generally, when the channels are jointly Gaussian with a known covariance, P(correct) is a single multivariate-normal orthant probability. Psychophysicists compute these integrals exactly with 1-D quadrature. They do not sample.
**Recipe.**
(a) *Ideal-observer baseline.* The Bayes-optimal accuracy for this task is 100%, so the structure of the network's 4.7% error is what needs explaining.
(b) *Treat the network as a noisy order-statistic observer.* Write the logit margin for the true class j*, compared with the strongest competitor k, as a smooth function of the order statistics (x_(1), x_(2), x_(3); gap g = x_(1) − x_(2); position offsets). Fit or derive an effective "internal noise" σ_eff(Δt), where Δt is the lag between the positions of the max and the 2nd max. Fourier-memory phase blur will make σ_eff grow with Δt.
(c) Accuracy ≈ E_{order stats}[Φ(margin / σ_eff)]. This is a psychometric function of the gap x_(2) − x_(3) and of x_(1) − x_(2). The joint density of the top-3 order statistics of 10 iid normals is known in closed form: 10·9·8 φ(a)φ(b)φ(c)Φ(c)^7 on a > b > c. Integrating over it is a 3-D quadrature, plus a sum over the 10·9 (max, 2nd-max) position pairs. That is cheap and has no Monte Carlo variance.
(d) Error decomposition: near-tie errors give a psychometric slope (a "discrimination threshold" in units of the gap), and "≤1 positive input" errors give a separate lapse term. P(≤1 of 10 positive) = 11/1024 ≈ 1.07%, which is exact and simply an upper bound on that lapse term.
**Why promising.** It gives an analytic error budget immediately (lapse plus threshold-limited errors) and is a natural surrogate or control variate for the exact cone computation in #2.

### 2. Threshold-linear network (TLN) theory: permitted/forbidden sets and piecewise-linear "gate-pattern" analysis  [promise: very high, feasibility: high]
**Citations.** Hahnloser, Seung & Slotine (2003) "Permitted and forbidden sets in symmetric threshold-linear networks", *Neural Computation* 15:621-638 (NeurIPS 2000 version: Hahnloser & Seung). Hahnloser (1998) "On the piecewise analysis of networks of linear threshold neurons", *Neural Networks* 11:691-697. Curto, Geneson & Morrison (2019) "Fixed points of competitive threshold-linear networks", *Neural Computation* 31:94-155 (arXiv:1804.00794). Morrison & Curto (2019) "Predicting neural network dynamics via graphical analysis" (arXiv:1804.01487).
**Idea.** A TLN is linear on each "support" (the set of active neurons) σ. A support is *permitted* if the restricted matrix (I − W)_σσ makes it stable or self-consistent. The fixed-point supports follow from sign conditions on Cramer's-rule determinants (Curto et al.), and "graph rules" constrain which supports can coexist. Permitted sets are closed under taking subsets and forbidden sets are closed under taking supersets.
**Recipe.**
(a) For our feed-forward-in-time map, a support *trajectory* (σ_1, …, σ_10) defines a product of masked matrices A = W_o D_10 W_h D_9 … D_1 W_i. Each trajectory also defines its polyhedral cone, given by the sign conditions "pre-activation > 0 iff unit is in σ_t". These are the Curto-style sign conditions, applied over time instead of at a fixed point.
(b) Use the subset-closure idea to prune the search: if a partial trajectory's cone is empty (an LP-feasibility check), every extension of it is empty too. Branch-and-bound over time steps finds the reachable cones. The positive-homogeneity property guarantees the cones are pointed at the origin, so each feasibility check is a homogeneous LP.
(c) Within each cone the decision is linear. The correct region is a further polyhedral sub-cone, and its Gaussian measure is an orthant probability in ≤ (number of active constraints) dimensions. Evaluate it with Genz's QMC/SOV algorithm (Genz 1992, *J. Comp. Graph. Stat.* 1:141-149), whose error is far smaller than plain MC at equal compute. For low-dimensional cones, use exact solid-angle formulas.
(d) Robustness to perturbed weights: support trajectories are discrete, so small perturbations move the cone boundaries but rarely change the reachable set. Reuse the same enumeration and re-evaluate the measures.
**Risk.** The number of reachable cones can be large (16 units x 10 steps). Prioritise them by Gaussian mass. Expect a long tail, and handle the tail with stratified MC (see #8).

### 3. Laplace-transform / scale-invariant temporal memory and "time cells"  [promise: high for understanding, medium for accuracy]
**Citations.** Shankar & Howard (2012) "A scale-invariant internal representation of time", *Neural Computation* 24:134-193. Howard et al. (2014) "A unified mathematical framework for coding time, space, and sequences in the hippocampal region", *J. Neurosci.* 34:4692-4707. Shankar & Howard (2013) "Optimally fuzzy temporal memory", *JMLR* 14:3785-3812.
**Idea.** A bank of leaky integrators with decay rates s holds F(s,t) = ∫ e^{−s(t−τ)} f(τ) dτ, the Laplace transform of the input history. "Time cells" approximately invert it (Post's formula), which yields a fuzzy log-time reconstruction of when events happened. The temporal resolution degrades in a Weber-like way with elapsed time.
**Recipe.** Our network uses complex s: a damped Fourier/Laplace bank with |λ| ≈ 0.84-0.95 and four harmonics. Treat h_10 restricted to the oscillatory subspace as F(s_k, ·) evaluated at the event "max occurred". Then:
(a) The readout W_o works like an inverse-Laplace / time-cell layer. Project the rows of W_o onto the eigenbasis and check whether each output logit j is a "time cell" tuned to lag 10 − j. Plot the implied temporal receptive fields, which are the logits as a function of the lag of a unit impulse injected into the memory.
(b) Derive the resolution of the time readout analytically. With 4 harmonics and decays |λ|^Δt, adjacent-lag discriminability d'(Δt) ≈ |∂F/∂Δt| / σ_eff, which shrinks as |λ|^Δt. This predicts *which* position pairs are confused (adjacent positions, older events) and feeds σ_eff(Δt) into the psychometric model of #1.
(c) The "record detector" resets the memory, like the "event onset" of Howard's model. The 2nd-max position has to be recovered from the "time since the last record" plus the comparator history, which is the same ingredient Howard's sequence-memory models use.

### 4. Legendre Memory Units / HiPPO: optimal delay-line memories with closed-form state-space matrices  [promise: medium-high, feasibility: high]
**Citations.** Voelker, Kajić & Eliasmith (2019) "Legendre Memory Units: continuous-time representation in recurrent neural networks", *NeurIPS 32*. Voelker & Eliasmith (2018) "Improving spiking dynamical networks: accurate delays, higher-order synapses, and time cells", *Neural Computation* 30:569-609 (Padé-approximant delay network, time cells). Gu, Dao, Ermon, Rudra & Ré (2020) "HiPPO: Recurrent memory with optimal polynomial projections", *NeurIPS 33*.
**Idea.** A d-dimensional linear system (A,B) can be designed so that its state holds the coefficients of a sliding window of input history projected onto Legendre polynomials (LMU), or onto other measures (HiPPO-LegS/LagT/Fourier). The memory error is controlled analytically by d and by the window length.
**Recipe.** Fit the *gated-average* linearization of W_h (restricted to the memory subspace) to the HiPPO-FouT/LMU family. Equivalently, compute which measure ω(τ) over the past makes our eigenbasis the optimal projection. That gives a closed-form "memory kernel" K(τ), and the network's knowledge of "when the max happened" becomes a known function approximation problem with a computable truncation error. Use that error as the analytic σ_eff(Δt) in #1 and #3. This also explains *why* the harmonic eigenvalue series appears: it is a truncated Fourier basis (HiPPO-FouT), and our measured periods (8.4, 4.3, 2.9, 2 ≈ 8.4/k) match k = 1..4.

### 5. Fisher memory curve: linear memory traces in non-normal dynamical systems  [promise: medium-high, feasibility: high]
**Citation.** Ganguli, Huh & Sompolinsky (2008) "Memory traces in dynamical systems", *PNAS* 105:18970-18975.
**Idea.** For h_{t+1} = W h_t + v s_t + noise, the Fisher Memory Curve J(k) = v^T W^{kT} C^{-1} W^k v measures how much SNR about the input k steps ago survives in the current state. Non-normal (hidden feedforward) structure can raise the total memory to N, whereas normal networks are limited to 1.
**Recipe.** Within each frequent gate regime (#2), compute J(k) for the "max-event" input direction. Use the *input distribution itself* as the noise source: other inputs' contributions to h act as structured noise with covariance C computable from the Gaussian inputs and the gate regime. J(k) then gives d'(k) for detecting or locating events at lag k. Plug it into Φ(d'/2)-type error formulas (#1, #6). Also compute the Schur decomposition of the gated W_h. The non-normal (feedforward) part tells you how the comparator → memory → readout chain is organised, which is a clean "how it computes" story.

### 6. Population-decoding theory: linear discriminant / Fisher-information error rates  [promise: medium-high, feasibility: high]
**Citations.** Averbeck, Latham & Pouget (2006) "Neural correlations, population coding and computation", *Nat. Rev. Neurosci.* 7:358-366. Seung & Sompolinsky (1993) "Simple models for reading neuronal population codes", *PNAS* 90:10749-10753. Moreno-Bote et al. (2014) "Information-limiting correlations", *Nat. Neurosci.* 17:1410-1417.
**Idea.** If class-conditional population responses r | c are approximately Gaussian with means μ_c and covariance Σ, a linear decoder's pairwise error is Φ(−d'_{ck}/2), with d'^2 = Δμ^T Σ^{-1} Δμ. The total error follows from a union / multiclass orthant integral. Information-limiting correlations are the covariance components along Δμ.
**Recipe.**
(a) Condition on the "class" pair (max position i, 2nd-max position j) and on a coarse gap bin. Estimate μ, Σ of h_10, or compute them semi-analytically using the closed-form ReLU Gaussian moments in #7.
(b) For the actual readout W_o, the logit differences are linear in h_10, so they are Gaussian under this approximation. P(correct | class) = P(all 9 logit differences > 0) is a 9-D MVN orthant probability, which Genz handles quickly.
(c) The "information-limiting correlations" analysis explains the observation that per-neuron labels give only 54% accuracy: the decision lives in the small cross-neuron covariance structure along Δμ, so single-neuron tuning curves miss it. Report d' contributed by Σ-whitened directions versus per-neuron directions.
**Caveat.** The Gaussian approximation of h_10 breaks down for near-tie inputs, which are exactly where the errors are. Use it as a control variate, not as the final answer.

### 7. Dynamical mean-field / Gaussian moment propagation through ReLU layers (low-rank RNN theory)  [promise: medium, feasibility: medium-high]
**Citations.** Mastrogiuseppe & Ostojic (2018) "Linking connectivity, dynamics and computations in low-rank recurrent neural networks", *Neuron* 99:609-623. Dubreuil, Valente, Beiran, Mastrogiuseppe & Ostojic (2022) "The role of population structure in computations through neural dynamics", *Nat. Neurosci.* 25:783-794. Beiran et al. (2021) "Shaping dynamics with multiple populations in low-rank RNNs", *Neural Computation* 33:1572-1615. Closed-form rectified-Gaussian moments: Cho & Saul (2009) arc-cosine kernel, *NeurIPS*.
**Idea.** Low-rank RNNs reduce to a few latent variables κ_r(t). With Gaussian inputs, the pre-activations are Gaussian at each step *conditioned on the latents*, so the dynamics close as ODEs or maps on the latent moments using E[ReLU(z)] and E[ReLU(z)ReLU(z')] (arc-cosine kernels).
**Recipe.** Do an SVD of W_h. If a few modes dominate (running max + 4 complex pairs = about 9 dimensions), track the latents. Condition on the first t inputs being summarised by a small sufficient statistic (running max M_t, time since max, current 2nd max S_t). The pre-activation for the new input x_{t+1} is then *exactly* Gaussian in x_{t+1}, so the one-step update of the network is a closed-form 1-D integral. This yields a Markov-chain-style exact recursion on (M_t, S_t, lag) on a grid, combined with the network's response. That is, it is a semi-analytic density propagation, the "neural field" version of computing accuracy. Its accuracy depends on how well (M, S, lags) summarise h_t. Check this by measuring the residual variance of h_t given those variables.

### 8. Fixed-point / linearization reverse engineering, and switching linear dynamical systems (SLDS)  [promise: medium, feasibility: high]
**Citations.** Sussillo & Barak (2013) "Opening the black box: low-dimensional dynamics in high-dimensional recurrent neural networks", *Neural Computation* 25:626-649. Maheswaranathan, Williams, Golub, Ganguli & Sussillo (2019) "Reverse engineering recurrent networks for sentiment classification reveals line attractor dynamics", *NeurIPS 32*. Smith, Linderman & Sussillo (2021) "Reverse engineering recurrent neural networks with Jacobian switching linear dynamical systems", *NeurIPS 34* (arXiv:2111.01256). Linderman et al. (2017) "Bayesian learning and inference in recurrent switching linear dynamical systems", *AISTATS*.
**Idea.** Find (slow) fixed points, linearise around them, and read off the eigenmodes. For piecewise-linear nets, the exact representation is a switching LDS whose discrete state is the gate pattern. Maheswaranathan et al. show that input-driven RNNs are understood via the input-conditioned "fixed point manifold" and the eigenmodes of the Jacobian along it.
**Recipe.** Because there are no biases, the only autonomous fixed point is 0 (or a ray, if an eigenvalue equals 1). Instead, consider the *input-conditioned* fixed points h*(x) = ReLU(W_h h* + W_i x) for a constant x, and the Jacobian D(σ) W_h per gate regime σ. Cluster the observed (σ_t) into a handful of SLDS regimes (for example: "new record", "below max and above 2nd", "below 2nd"). These correspond to the *comparator* states. Per regime, report the eigenvalues and how they rotate the Fourier memory. Regime switching is a small finite-state automaton, and the accuracy calculation becomes a sum over automaton paths (as in #2, but coarse-grained). Use the SLDS paths as **strata** for stratified Monte Carlo. Even when the analytic sum is incomplete, stratifying by the regime path, with variance-optimal allocation, beats plain MC at equal compute.

### 9. Neural integrators and line attractors (the running-max neuron)  [promise: medium for understanding, low for accuracy]
**Citations.** Seung (1996) "How the brain keeps the eyes still", *PNAS* 93:13339-13344. Seung, Lee, Reis & Tank (2000) "Stability of the memory of eye position in a recurrent network of conductance-based model neurons", *Neuron* 26:617-631. Goldman (2009) "Memory without feedback in a neural network", *Neuron* 61:621-634 (feedforward/non-normal integrators).
**Idea.** Persistent memory needs an eigenvalue of 1 (tuned positive feedback). A mistuned eigenvalue λ ≠ 1 gives leak or drift with time constant 1/|1 − λ|. Goldman shows that integration can instead be done by a hidden feedforward chain.
**Recipe.** Measure the self-loop and effective eigenvalue of the running-max integrator in the "no new record" regime. The error of the stored max after k steps is (1 − λ)^k-scaled. Combine this with the comparator thresholds to get the *effective comparison* x_t vs λ^k·M. A mistuned integrator turns into a systematic bias: older maxima look smaller. That predicts a specific asymmetric error pattern (late near-ties are resolved in favour of the more recent value), which can be tested. It also feeds a deterministic bias term into the psychometric model of #1.

### 10. k-Winner-take-all circuits and order statistics in neural circuits  [promise: medium, feasibility: medium]
**Citations.** Majani, Erlanson & Abu-Mostafa (1989) "On the K-winners-take-all network", *NIPS 1*. Xie, Hahnloser & Seung (2002) "Selectively grouping neurons in recurrent networks of lateral inhibition", *Neural Computation* 14:2627-2646. Maass (2000) "On the computational power of winner-take-all", *Neural Computation* 12:2519-2535.
**Idea.** Lateral-inhibition TLNs select the top-k inputs. Their stable sets are exactly the k-subsets, and the selection margin (the gap needed for correct selection) is set by the inhibition and self-excitation strengths. Maass shows WTA plus linear gates is universal.
**Recipe.** View the task as a serial (temporal) 2-WTA: keep top-1 (M) and top-2 (S) online with the update rules "if x > M: S←M, M←x elif x > S: S←x". Check whether the network implements an *explicit* S register (a neuron tracking the running 2nd max) or only a time-stamp of when M occurred, plus the comparator history. The k-WTA margin analysis then gives the minimum resolvable gap δ(lag). Combined with the closed-form density of the top-3 order-statistic gaps, P(gap < δ), this gives a first-order analytic error rate. For iid N(0,1) with n = 10, the gaps x_(1) − x_(2) and x_(2) − x_(3) have known densities (e.g. via ∫ n(n−1)(n−2)φ(a)φ(b)φ(c)Φ(c)^{n−3}), and for small δ, P(gap < δ) ≈ f_gap(0)·δ.

### 11. Ideal-observer analysis with "neural noise" replaced by input-induced variability  [promise: medium, feasibility: medium]
**Citations.** Geisler (2011) "Contributions of ideal observer theory to vision research", *Vision Res.* 51:771-781. Beck et al. (2012) "Not noisy, just wrong: the role of suboptimal inference in behavioral variability", *Neuron* 74:30-39.
**Idea.** Beck et al. argue that behavioural errors in deterministic networks come from *suboptimal (approximate) inference*, not from noise. The error is a deterministic function of the stimulus, and it looks like noise only once you marginalise over nuisance stimulus variables.
**Recipe.** This is exactly our situation: the network is deterministic, and its "noise" is the influence of the 8 non-top inputs and of the input magnitudes. Decompose the logit margin as m(x) = m_ideal(order stats) + ε(nuisance), where nuisance = (the other 8 values, the overall scale). Compute Var(ε | top-3) analytically: within a gate regime, ε is linear in the nuisance inputs, and conditioned on them being below x_(3) they are truncated normals with closed-form moments. That turns the deterministic network into a *psychometric observer with computable internal noise*, which is the input #1 needs. It also explains why per-neuron labels fail: the "noise" is suppressed by cancellations across neurons.

## Suggested pipeline (combining the top ideas)
1. **Exact skeleton (#2 + #8):** enumerate the high-mass gate-regime paths with LP pruning, using positive homogeneity to reduce everything to cones. For each path, compute the Gaussian cone measure of "correct" with Genz QMC or an exact low-dimensional solid angle.
2. **Analytic surrogate (#1 + #3/#4/#5 + #11):** model the logit margin as a psychometric function of the top-3 order statistics and the lag, with internal noise σ_eff(lag) derived from the Fourier-memory resolution and the nuisance-input variance. Integrate exactly against the order-statistic density. This gives an interpretable error budget: lapse (≤1 positive input, at most 11/1024 of inputs), threshold (near-tie), and bias (integrator leak).
3. **Variance reduction for the tail:** use the surrogate from step 2 as a control variate, and the regime paths from step 1 as strata, for the residual mass the exact enumeration doesn't cover. This is how to beat MC at equal compute and stay robust to weight perturbations: re-run step 1 per perturbed network, and reuse the surrogate's structure with refitted coefficients.
